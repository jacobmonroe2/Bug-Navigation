Project 2 · Autonomous navigation to a goal pose with obstacle avoidance · Source code

What the robot does

The robot is given a goal pose (x, y, θ) measured from where it starts and has to drive there on its own, going around anything in the way. It has no map: only its wheel odometry, its current Lidar scan and the goal. We implemented a Bug 2 algorithm. The robot drives along the m-line, the straight line from start to goal. When the Lidar shows an obstacle in the way, it follows the obstacle's edge. Once it is back on the m-line and closer to the goal than where it hit the obstacle, it leaves the obstacle and carries on along the line. Because the MBot-Omni can move in any direction, it translates without turning and only rotates to the goal heading at the very end.

State machine

                     blocked                       back on m-line & closer
 +---------------+ ------------> +-------------+ -------------------------+
 | DRIVE_TO_GOAL |               | FOLLOW_WALL |                          |
 +---------------+ <------------ +-------------+ <------------------------+
        |                               |
        | at goal position              | back at the hit point after
        v                               v going all the way around
 +---------------+                +------------------+
 | TURN_TO_GOAL  |                | GOAL_UNREACHABLE |
 +---------------+                +------------------+
        |
        | heading correct
        v
 +---------------+
 |    AT_GOAL    |
 +---------------+
StateBehaviour
0 · DRIVE_TO_GOALTravel along the m-line toward the goal at 0.25 to 0.35 m/s.
1 · FOLLOW_WALLProject 1 wall follower: travel along the obstacle's edge while holding a 0.27 m gap.
2 · TURN_TO_GOALRotate in place to the requested heading.
3 · AT_GOALStop and print the final odometry.
4 · GOAL_UNREACHABLEWent all the way around the obstacle without finding a way through, so stop.

The state is printed every control iteration together with the time, the pose, the distance to the goal and the shortest Lidar ray in the direction of travel, so transitions can be checked from the terminal.

Detecting obstacles

Checking only the single Lidar ray pointing where the robot is heading misses obstacles that sit just beside that ray. Instead we take the minimum ray inside a 70° slice centred on the direction of travel. If that ray is shorter than 0.40 m, and shorter than the distance to the goal so the object is really between us and the goal, the way is blocked and the robot switches to wall following. It goes around on the side away from the closest ray, so it does not take the long way around an off-centre box.

Discussion

  • What the real robot taught us. Our first version passed every simulated test but barely moved on the MBot. The log showed the wheels do not turn at all for commands under about 0.25 m/s, and the controller slowed to exactly those speeds near obstacles and near the goal. We changed it so the robot either moves at 0.25 to 0.35 m/s or stops, never crawls. On top of that, a stall check watches the odometry. If the robot is told to move but is not moving, it turns the command up in steps until it does.
  • Getting back on the line. After going around a box, the early version drove straight at the goal from wherever it was, which made a Y-shaped path. Now the robot aims at a point 0.10 m further along the m-line. Off the line, that pulls it back on, so it rejoins its original path right behind the obstacle and finishes in a straight line.
  • Following one obstacle at a time. The Project 1 wall follower treats the nearest Lidar ray as the wall. In a cluttered room the nearest ray keeps jumping between objects, and the robot shuttled back and forth. We group Lidar points closer than 0.45 m into one obstacle, since the robot cannot fit between them, and follow only the obstacle we started on.
  • Knowing when to give up. If the robot comes back to where it first hit the obstacle, there is no way around it to the goal, for example when the goal is outside the room. It stops in GOAL_UNREACHABLE instead of circling forever.
  • Bug 2 vs. Bug 1. Bug 1 circles every obstacle completely before leaving at the closest point, which is safe but slow. Bug 2 leaves as soon as the m-line is re-crossed closer to the goal, which gives much shorter paths on the boxes used in the demo course.
  • Testing without the robot. We wrote a 2D simulator with ray-cast Lidar (sim/run_sim.py) and a "realistic" robot model built from our robot's logs: wheels that stall at low speed, noisy Lidar and communication delay. The navigation code reaches the goal in all 9 reachable test worlds with both robot models, without collisions, and correctly gives up on the 2 unreachable ones. Across 12 different robot variations it still sometimes gives up too early in a narrow doorway or a very cluttered room when the Lidar is noisy, which is the main thing we would improve next.
